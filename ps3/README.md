2.2.c
When we increase the number of variables from 401 to 801, the time required for the Jacobians increases from 0.1 to 0.9 seconds (a factor of 9), while Klein's method increases from 0.3 to 1.8 seconds (a factor of 6). When we instead increase the number to 1201, these increase to 1.9 and 5.0 seconds, respectively, corresponding to increases by factors of approximately 2.1 and 2.8. This is because solving larger linearised models is costly.

The Jacobian calculations exhibit a larger relative increase in the first case, whereas Klein's method grows faster in the second. We know in general that, for the Jacobian, keeping N_e fixed, the computational cost grows approximately quadratically with the number of variables, whereas in Klein's method, the costs grow at a cubic rate with the dimensions of the system. We would therefore expect Klein's method to become more and more expensive relative to the Jacobian case. What we observe is broadly consistent with what we just stated. However, it is true that the observed growth factors vary across grid sizes and do not exactly follow these theoretical scaling rates.

2.3.b
Every call of solve_pranc() calibrates β so that the steady state keeps r = 4% and the same K and C. In the steady state, with C_{t+1} = C_t, equation (4) gives ln(βR) = γ(N-P). N (the newborn term) does not depend on income risk, whereas P (the precautionary term) grows with σ_e^2. As with more income risk HH want to save more for a given β, to keep K at the same level they must be less patient, that is, β must decrease.

2.3.c
Aggregate dynamics do not change much with income risk. When σ_e increases from 0 to 0.3, consumption on impact decreases from 0.553% to 0.541%, while the peak capital response increases from 1.025% to 1.040%. The IRFs remain almost identical across the three specifications. Under CARA preferences, all HH have the same MPC, so the wealth distribution does not directly affect aggregate consumption. Here, the differences in aggregate dynamics are mainly driven by the precautionary-saving mechanism. After a positive TFP shock, wages increase. Then, the absolute variance of the idiosyncratic labor income increases as well, which leads to a higher precautionary-saving motive. When σ_e is higher, this effect is larger: HH save more and consume less. As a result of these dynamics, capital accumulation is higher. Overall, it must be however noted that these effects remain quite small, since the precautionary savings motive is quite small compared to aggregate consumption.

3.4.c
In this economy, with σ_e = 0 HH still differ by age and therefore wealth. Newborns enter the economy with zero assets, while surviving HH accumulate savings over their lifetimes. Since βR>1, unconstrained HH have an increasing consumption path as they age, so older HH generally have more wealth and consume more than newborn HH. This economy is not the RA because HH die at some point and are replaced by newborns with a = 0. Even though individual consumption increases over the lifetime of HH that survive in a given period, aggregate consumption remains constant in steady state, despite positive consumption growth at the individual level.
